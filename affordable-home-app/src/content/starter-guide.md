# The Essex County Affordable Housing Starter Guide

### What to apply for, in what order, and what you'll need

**Free. Made in Essex County. Not a government agency.**

*Counselors, agencies, and elected officials: please forward this freely.*

---

## Before you read anything else

Apply to many lists at the same time. Do not pick one and wait.

Most affordable housing lists in New Jersey fill by random lottery. Applying early does not move you up. Applying to more lists gives you more chances. Put your name everywhere you qualify, and keep applying as new lists open.

That one habit separates people who get housed from people who wait years for nothing. Everything else in this guide supports it.

---

## 1. How this works, and how long it takes

Affordable housing runs on waiting lists, and most lists stay closed most of the time. When one opens, it can close within days. Across all of New Jersey, only nine Section 8 lists opened for even a single day in a recent three-year stretch. Almost half of those closed inside a week.

So the game is simple. Get your paperwork ready now. Apply the day a list opens. Apply to every list you can reach.

Two things decide where you land: your household income, and luck in the lottery. Once your name comes up, you get a short window, often 60 to 120 days, to find an apartment and pass an inspection. Receiving a voucher is step one. Using it before it expires is the harder step, so start looking the moment you are selected.

Waits are long. Households in New Jersey spend an average of about 31 months on a voucher waiting list before a voucher comes through. Plan around that. Apply anyway. The list you join today is the one that pays off in year three.

## 2. The three doors

New Jersey runs affordable housing through three separate systems. They have different rules, different applications, and different waits. Most people find one and stop. Use all three.

### Door 1: Vouchers you take with you

A voucher pays your landlord the gap between roughly 30% of your income and the rent, up to a ceiling. You find the apartment yourself on the private market.

**Federal Section 8 (Housing Choice Voucher).** Run by local housing authorities and by the state. A federal voucher moves with you anywhere in the country.

**NJ State Rental Assistance Program (SRAP).** Works the same way, but the money comes from the state. SRAP only works inside New Jersey, and you must have lived in New Jersey for at least six months before applying.

Both use the same application portal at the state level, and both go through lottery.

### Door 2: Buildings where the rent is already lower

These apartments come with the building, not with you. You apply to the management company, not to a housing authority.

- **LIHTC (tax credit) apartments.** Built with federal tax credits in exchange for capped rents. Some have short waits. A few have none.
- **Section 202.** Buildings for seniors 62 and older.
- **Section 811.** Buildings for adults with disabilities.
- **Project-based Section 8.** The subsidy stays attached to specific units in specific buildings.

Almost nobody checks Door 2, which is exactly why it is worth your time. Find these buildings through the New Jersey Housing Resource Center at njhousing.gov or by calling 1-877-428-8844, and through HUD's online Resource Locator.

### Door 3: Mount Laurel (municipal) units

New Jersey law requires every town to create its share of affordable housing. That produces income-restricted apartments and for-sale homes inside ordinary market-rate developments, including in towns with no housing authority at all.

Companies called administrative agents run these lists for the towns. Two large ones cover much of Essex County:

- **CGP&H**, at affordablehomesnewjersey.com. One preliminary application creates a profile that can put you on many town lists at once. Start here.
- **Piazza & Associates**, at piazzanj.com. Apply property by property.

Selection happens by lottery, not by who applied first.

**These three doors do not compete with each other.** Walk through all of them.

## 3. Do you qualify?

Eligibility runs off Area Median Income, or AMI. For most programs here, that means the Newark HUD Metro area: Essex, Morris, Sussex, and Union counties together.

Your limit depends on how many people live in your household. Income means gross annual income before taxes, for everyone in the home, not your take-home pay.

**HUD income limits, Newark area. Effective May 1, 2026.**

| People in household | Extremely Low (30%) | Very Low (50%) | Low (80%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $29,070 | $48,450 | $77,500 |
| 2 | $33,240 | $55,400 | $88,600 |
| 3 | $37,380 | $62,300 | $99,650 |
| 4 | $41,520 | $69,200 | $110,700 |
| 5 | $44,850 | $74,750 | $119,600 |
| 6 | $48,180 | $80,300 | $128,450 |
| 7 | $51,510 | $85,850 | $137,300 |
| 8 | $54,810 | $91,350 | $146,150 |

Most vouchers require income at or below the Very Low column. Federal rules push at least three out of four new vouchers to households at or below the Extremely Low column. Many tax credit apartments use a 60% level, which works out to about 1.2 times the Very Low figure.

**Mount Laurel units use a different table.** Those follow state limits for Region 2, which covers Essex, Morris, Union, and Warren counties. As of June 15, 2026, a one-person household qualified as moderate income up to $77,200, and a four-person household up to $110,240. Ask the administrative agent for the current figures when you apply.

Both tables change every year, usually in spring. Run your household through the free checker at **homereach.site** and it will compare you against the current numbers.

## 4. Who to apply to in Essex County

Seven housing authorities operate in Essex County. They do not share waiting lists. Being on one does nothing for the others.

**Local housing authorities:**

- Newark Housing Authority, the largest in the county
- Housing Authority of the City of East Orange
- Irvington Township Housing Authority
- Orange Housing Authority
- Bloomfield Public Housing Agency
- Montclair Housing Authority
- West Orange Public Housing Agency

Most accept applicants who live outside their town, so apply to all seven, not just your own. Search for each by name to reach its official site. Confirm you are on a real housing authority page before you enter any personal information.

**The state program.** New Jersey's Department of Community Affairs runs a Section 8 and SRAP program open to residents of any county. Applications open rarely, close fast, and select by computer lottery. Check status at waitlistcheck.com and search for the New Jersey DCA program. The pre-application comes in 90 languages. For a disability accommodation, call (609) 292-4080 and choose option 9.

Expect competition. In one recent opening, more than 141,000 households applied for roughly 20,000 spots on the list. Apply anyway. The odds are bad and the cost is zero.

**Preferences can move you up.** State rules give the highest preference to veterans, followed by households experiencing homelessness, people with disabilities, survivors of domestic violence, and local residents. If any apply to you, say so on the application and bring documentation.

**Where to check for open lists.** Waitlist status changes without warning, so no printed guide can keep up. Check affordablehousingonline.com, which tracks openings by county and sends alerts. Check each housing authority's site monthly.

### New lotteries are coming through 2035

New Jersey towns must plan for new affordable homes between 2025 and 2035. Suburban Essex towns carry the largest new-construction obligations, including West Orange, Millburn, Livingston, Fairfield, West Caldwell, Maplewood, Cedar Grove, South Orange, Glen Ridge, Roseland, Verona, Essex Fells, North Caldwell, and Caldwell.

These are towns that have built very little affordable housing before. As those units get built, new lotteries will open. Watch each town's website, plus the CGP&H and Piazza sites.

Newark, East Orange, Irvington, Orange, Belleville, Bloomfield, Montclair, and Nutley carry obligations to repair and preserve existing affordable homes rather than build new ones. If you already rent in one of those towns and your unit is in poor condition, ask your municipal housing office about rehabilitation programs.

## 5. Gather your documents once

Every application asks for nearly the same paperwork. Assemble one folder, photograph each item with your phone, and you can finish an application in minutes instead of weeks. When a list opens for four days, that folder is the difference.

**Identity, for everyone in the household**
- ☐ Photo ID for each adult
- ☐ Birth certificates for children
- ☐ Social Security cards for everyone

**Income**
- ☐ Last four to six pay stubs for each working adult
- ☐ Most recent federal tax return with W-2s
- ☐ Award letters for Social Security, SSI, SSDI, unemployment, pension, child support, or TANF
- ☐ Last two months of bank statements, checking and savings

**Where you live now**
- ☐ Current lease
- ☐ Recent utility bill showing your address
- ☐ Landlord's name, address, and phone number

**If they apply to you**
- ☐ Divorce or custody paperwork
- ☐ Documentation of disability, if you are requesting an accommodation
- ☐ DD-214, for veterans

Keep the folder current. An application built on last year's pay stubs gets rejected. Refresh your pay stubs every few months.

## 6. Your first week

**Day 1.** Run the eligibility checker at homereach.site. Write down which programs match your household.

**Day 2.** Build the document folder above. Scan or photograph everything. Store it somewhere you can reach from your phone.

**Day 3.** Set up an email address you check often and will keep for years. Lottery notices and waitlist updates arrive by email, and some applications require one. Use an address you will still have in three years.

**Day 4.** Apply to every open list you qualify for. Create your CGP&H profile at affordablehomesnewjersey.com and join every Essex County town list it offers. Save every confirmation number in one place.

**Day 5.** Search njhousing.gov for buildings near you and call the management companies directly. Ask two questions: is your waiting list open, and how do I apply?

**Day 6.** Call a HUD-certified housing counselor. Section 10 has the numbers. The call is free.

**Every month after.** Check for newly opened lists. Set a recurring reminder on your phone.

## 7. Staying on the list

Getting on a list is the easy part. People lose their place for reasons that have nothing to do with whether they qualify.

**Update your address the day it changes, in writing.** Housing authorities notify you by mail. One returned letter can drop you from a list you waited three years to reach the top of. Do not rely on a phone call. Send it in writing and keep a copy.

**Report changes in income, phone number, and household size.** Someone moving in or out changes the size of unit you qualify for, and sometimes your eligibility.

**Answer every letter by the deadline printed on it.** Silence gets recorded as no longer interested.

**Save your confirmation numbers.** You will need them any time you call about your application.

**Recertify when asked.** Many lists purge applicants who do not respond to an update request.

**Remember the lottery.** Your application date does not set your position on most New Jersey lists. That cuts both ways. Applying late costs you nothing, and applying early guarantees you nothing.

## 8. Nobody should charge you

Applying for affordable housing is free. Every program in this guide costs nothing to apply to. Housing authorities are barred from charging a Section 8 application fee.

Walk away from anyone who:

- Runs a website ending in .com or .net that copies a government seal and asks for a fee
- Charges to submit your application or to "get you on the list"
- Promises to move you up a waiting list or guarantees you a voucher
- Offers to sell or transfer a voucher to you. Vouchers cannot be transferred, and that offer is always a scam
- Texts or emails saying you were "selected" and asks you to click a link and pay
- Advertises "guaranteed approval" on social media
- Asks you to wire a deposit before you have seen the apartment and signed a lease
- Asks for your Social Security number on a call you did not place

Housing counseling from HUD-certified agencies is also free. If someone charges you for it, find another agency.

Report scams at reportfraud.ftc.gov, to the HUD Office of Inspector General, and to your local housing authority.

## 9. Know your rights

**A landlord cannot refuse your voucher.** New Jersey law makes source of lawful income a protected category, and that includes Section 8, SRAP, and temporary rental assistance. A landlord cannot turn you down only because you would pay with a voucher, and cannot advertise "no Section 8." The state has brought repeated enforcement actions on this, including in Essex County.

If it happens to you, write down the date, the address, the person's name, and what they said. Save the listing or text message. Then call the New Jersey Division on Civil Rights at **(866) 405-3050** or file at NJCivilRights.gov.

**You have the right to an accommodation.** If a disability affects your ability to apply, attend an appointment, or use a unit, you can request a reasonable accommodation. Ask in writing.

**Fees have limits.** State rules restrict what affordable-unit landlords can charge on top of rent, including parking and amenity fees. If a fee looks wrong, ask the administrative agent before you sign.

## 10. If you are facing eviction right now

This guide plans years ahead, and waiting lists take years. If you could lose your housing this month, you need different help today.

- **NJ 2-1-1.** Dial 211 or visit nj211.org for emergency housing and rental assistance.
- **Legal Services of New Jersey.** Call 1-888-576-5529 for free legal help with landlord-tenant matters. Call before your court date, not after.
- **DCA Homelessness Prevention Program.** Help for households facing eviction who can sustain rent afterward. Ask about it at nj.gov/dca.
- **Essex County Division of Housing and Community Development.** Local emergency resources.

Do not skip a court date. Missing one can cost you your home even when you had a strong case.
